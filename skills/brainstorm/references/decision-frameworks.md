# Decision frameworks (for the converge / decide phase)

Distilled from decision-making practice (Bezos, Annie Duke, Ben Horowitz, et al., via Lenny's
decision-process skill). Apply the one or two lenses that fit the decision in front of you —
don't recite the whole list.

## Reversible vs. irreversible (two-way / one-way doors)
Jeff Bezos: Type 1 decisions are consequential and hard to reverse — one-way doors; slow down,
get them right. Type 2 are changeable — two-way doors; move fast, decide and adjust. The first
question on any decision: **how hard is this to undo?** Most decisions are more reversible than
they feel — treating a two-way door like a one-way door is a common, expensive mistake.

## Cost of delay vs. cost of being wrong
Ask: "What's the cost of waiting another week to decide? What's the cost of deciding wrong?"
When the cost of delay exceeds the cost of a reversible mistake, decide now. Hesitation itself is
a choice with a price (Ben Horowitz: the worst thing a leader does is hesitate on the next
decision because both options look bad).

## Make the implicit explicit
Annie Duke: write down the assumptions behind a gut feeling. Intuition isn't worthless — but if
you never make it explicit, you never learn when it was wrong. State the assumptions out loud so
they can be checked, and so a future review can see why the call was made.

## "What would change my mind?"
Name, before deciding, the specific information or outcome that would flip the choice. If such
info exists and is cheap to get quickly — get it. If it doesn't exist or can't be gotten in time,
that's the signal to stop gathering and take the leap. This is the antidote to analysis paralysis.

## High-conviction leaps
Some decisions can't be solved with data (Brandon Chu). When you've explored honestly and the
data runs out, name it as a judgment call, take the leap, and own the accountability — don't keep pretending more analysis will resolve it.

## Decide → commit → don't relitigate
Once decided, commit fully even if you weren't 100% sure ("disagree and commit"). Reopening a
settled decision without new information is a tax — it drains energy and erodes the choice. Tie
this to the "what would change my mind" note: only reopen if that specific thing actually changes.

## Single accountable owner
For decisions involving other people, name one accountable decision-maker (DACI: Driver,
Approver, Contributor, Informed). Diffused ownership → no decision. For Devon's solo calls, this
collapses to: own it explicitly rather than letting it drift.

## Common traps to flag
- Analysis paralysis — gathering more data when enough already exists to decide.
- Treating all decisions with equal rigor — over-engineering reversible ones.
- Relitigating settled decisions without new information.
- Implicit assumptions — deciding on gut without recording the reasoning.
- Anchoring on the first option, or on the option already emotionally preferred.
